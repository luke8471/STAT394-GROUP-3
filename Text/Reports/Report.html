A Review of A Multivariate Approach to the Study of Orichalcum ingots from the Underwater Gela’s Archaeological Site

Gaia Smith & Alice Thompson

1. Introduction

1.1 Significance of this archaeological find and Gela

The historic Gela, in Sicily Italy has been a treasure trove of archaeological finds for generations. This ancient city was strategically positioned at the crux of major maritime trade routes, which made it a populous and successful trading port (Angelucci, 2026). Consequently, ships looking to trade goods like pottery, metals, and grain have traveled, and sunk, around Gela for centuries.

A shipwreck off the coast continues to provide significant archaeological finds to this day. This discovery dates back to 1988 (Nutter, 2025), where it was first stumbled upon by divers near Bulala beach. We are exploring one of the most significant finds from this wreck; A veritable hoard of the fabled metal, Orichalcum.

The discovery of 40 orichalcum ingots in 2017 was a monumental one. There had never been a discovery of this quantity in one place anywhere in the world. There was also evidence of consistent casting processes, with overall similarities suggesting a rudimentary mold production. This was likely a hole dug in the ground and molten alloy was poured in the indentation to solidify. The morphology of the found ingots suggested cast-diverse technologies.

2.2 What is orichalcum?

Orichalcum is defined as a copper alloy with zinc content as the principal components. A widely varying quantity of lead and tin among other trace elements are expected. There is no consistent proportion to the amount of copper and zinc found, but the overall composition and relative amounts of principal elemental components can be used to deduce the rough date and origin of a sample. The modern day equivalent is brass.

2.3 Summary of Collection Techniques

In the initial analysis of the orichalcum ingots, X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy via a portable spectrometer (Caponetti, Armetta, Martino, et al., 2017) was used. The subject paper includes that data and further investigates the composition of each ingot using more invasive and refined elemental analysis.

For further testing, and the subject of this paper, Caponetti, Armetta, Brusca, et al. (2017) chose to test elemental compositions using Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-MS) of each of the 38 orichalcum ingots. ICP-OES was used to obtain measures for the primary elements above 1000 parts per million (PPM) while ICP-MS was used for the more difficult to measure trace elements below 1000 PPM. Both techniques are destructive and require the extraction of ~20mg of the ingots by drilling a micro-well into each ingot to obtain material for testing.

2.4 Scope of This Multivariate Analysis

This multivariate analysis includes manipulations of the data collected from the ICP-OES and ICP-MS chemometric analysis, it does not include the data about physical characteristics of the ingots or any correlation analysis of those variables.

We have chosen to reuse data because the orichalcum ingot archaeological find near Gela, Italy is a literally one of a kind find, we do not have the resources or the belief that it would be ethical to conduct excessive destructive testing on artifacts of the like that may never be found again. Therefore, we have used Caponetti, Armetta, Martino, et al. (2017) ICP-OES and ICP-MS data of 38 orichalcum ingots.

Our goal is to create and present a comprehensive, comprehensible, reproducible, and replicable multivariate analysis of the data obtained from chemometric elemental analysis of 38 orichalcum ingots to determine if there is coherent clustering between samples that are consistent with different distances and linkages.

We will be excluding the Cu/Zn ratio variable in the source table as we chose to analyse the linkage of all elements between each other, not between Cu/Zn ratios and other elements.

3.2 QQ-plots & Henze-Zirkler Test For Multivariate Normality

We first created and visually assessed the QQ-normal plots of table 1 to see if data transformation would be appropriate to manage potential outliers and standardisation issues.

As this was before there was any scaling performed, we opted to display the elements individually to better display each elements influence.

Primary Elements

Cu roughly follows the normal QQ-line, indicating it may follow a normal distribution. Fe and Pb are similarly distributed to Cu, but show slight deviation from QQ-line in the upper and lower tails. Zn has clear deviation from the QQ-line in the upper tail.

Trace Elements

Ag, Co, and Ni roughly follow the QQ-line indicating some evidence for normality. All other trace elements have blatant deviations from the normal line, particularly in the upper tail, likely due to previously discussed potential outliers. The relatively small sample size of 38, the tiny concentrations relative to the main elements, and the small amount of material taken/used for this material analysis technique are all 3 factors that could and likely would be influencing the data.

Data Transformation

We tried a simple log transformation witch resulted in a few zero values which was of some concern, as log(0) is undefined and can influence towards non-normality in multivariate data, particularly in small datasets like this one. We also had negative values. These were not an issue because we later chose metrics which are calculated from coordinate distances for clustering which can handle negative values.

Zero values were compensated for by instead transforming using log(\(\mathcal x\) + 1), where \(\mathcal{x}\) is our data.

Henze-Zirkler test established if there was preliminary evidence for multivariate normality.

The QQ-plot of the log(\(\mathcal x\) + 1) showed slight improvement overall. Multiple trace elements which had big upper skews now follow the theoretical line better with As, V, and Cr in particular following the theoretical line better.

There is still evidence of some elements not following assumptions of normality with Sr being the most prominent example, however, after the log(\(\mathcal x\) + 1) transformation and considering the small sample size of the data set and most elements roughly following the line. We concluded that the assumption of normality would need further testing to evaluate, but appears to hold.

Ni (nickel) wen from widest to having an interquartile range smaller then the average, while Sb (antimony) went from largest range to again becoming much closer to the average. Overall we can clearly see that the standardisation and log(\(\mathcal x\) + 1) transformation have greatly added in the interpretability of our EDA graphs. We note that Sr appears to have 4 unusual values/outliers.

The Henze-Zirkler test stipulates

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}_0 :& \text{ The data follow a multivariate normal distribution}, \\ \mathcal{H}_1 :& \text{ The data do not follow a multivariate normal distribution}. \end{aligned} \]

This generated a p-value=0.069, suggesting that there is insufficient evidence to reject that the data follow a multivariate normal distribution. However, the QQ-plot shows many variables with evidence of non-normality and/or significant outliers that we decided further testing was warranted before determining if multivariate normality is an appropriate statistical assumption.

Data manipulation by Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) was determined to be the next best step towards multivariate analysis.

3.3 MAD

MAD was used because it is a robust, outlier-resistant, measure of variability. The log(\(\mathcal x\) +1) transformation somewhat fixed the skew and pulled the outliers in, but did not eliminate their mathematical influence. Interpreting variation in the data using the standard deviation which is highly affected by unusually high/low values or outliers would be statistically unsound.

Conducting the Henze Zirkler test for both the un-transformed data (p-value <0.001) and the transformed data (p-value 0.055) we can reject the null hypotheses and conclude that the data is only not-normal when it is not-transformed and outliers remain. After log(\(\mathcal x\) + 1) transformation and outlier removal using MAD, we can no longer safely reject the null hypotheses and have weak evidence (due to the p-value being barely above the cut off) that the processed data can be assumed to be multivariate normal.

2.5 Correlation plots before and after

Many of the element to element correlations change in degree and direction between the raw data and log(x + 1)-transformed data correlation matrices. Because Pearson correlations are not robust to changes due to outliers/heavily skewed data, we believe the transformed data correlation plot is a more accurate plot to make interpretations from.

Simple observations include a strong-positive correlation between Sb-As, Fe-Co, Ag-Bi, V-Cr, Cr-Rb, Cu-Bi, Cu-Ag, Sb-Cd, all of these pairs have an above 0.8 correlation value. Compared to the positive, strong-negative correlation can be found between Cd-Sn at ~-0.8 correlation value, with a multiple pairs having a somewhat strong-negative correlation in the negative 0.7 to -0.8 range. Interestingly the elements needed to create orichalchum/brass do not have strong correlations with one another.

This is a positive sign that the robustness of the transformation, i.e removing some of the outliers demonstrates their influence on the raw data.

Given the uncertainty about multivariate normality and the presence of negative values in our transformed data, a linear data analysis was chosen over a primary component analysis.

The UMAP PAM projection shows clear overlap groupings.

Interesting to note samples 16 and 15 are in different clusters but are close together on the coordinate space when compared to the dendrogram above. This makes intuitive sense as these two samples have a moderate degree of similarity to one another but both also are only moderately similar to their assigned clusters. On a UMAP projection, distance between points within a cluster can be seen as conveying the points similarity with each other however comparing far away points/clusters to each other can lead to issues due to the universal distance not being directly scaled. UMAP projections arn’t ideal for making interpretations from but the visualisation of PAM clusters allows us to see that all 3 clusters are somewhat close to one another.
